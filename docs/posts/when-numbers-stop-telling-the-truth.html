When Numbers Stop Telling the Truth

Social Science 43 · November 14, 2026

“Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.”
— Charles Goodhart, economist, 1975

If you look at a student with a 4.0 GPA, you would most likely assume that they are intelligent. The same goes for many other convincing numbers. A content creator with millions of followers is likely influential. A business generating millions in revenue is likely creating meaningful value. However, can we certainly know this based on numbers themselves? A student’s GPA does not measure their overall understanding and intelligence. A content creator’s follower account doesn’t measure their true influence. A business’ revenue does not measure its quality. If this is true, then why do we solely rely on numbers to tell us things that they do not truly measure?

Numbers can tell us how much, how often, when, and how many. But how do these measurable states relate to what actually matters? In some situations, the answer is actually yes. Measuring more concrete aspects of life can easily be defined by numbers. If you’re measuring someone’s accuracy on an exam, their score percentage undoubtedly tells you all you need to know. If you need to know how many sales a band made worldwide, the existing statistics give you your complete answer. However, when measuring broader aspects, such as a band’s popularity or a person’s intelligence, it becomes more difficult. Certain numbers can give us a great perspective of what the truth likely is, but it cannot tell us for sure.

This should remind us of the importance of considering that any small factor can make these numbers deceiving. For example, a student could have gotten terrible rest before the night of an exam. Even though they may not have tested well, they still could have understood the topic completely. The amount of questions they miss affects their grade, but it does not necessarily affect their proficiency upon something. Perhaps it tells us that their proficiency in remembering to get good sleep before exams is not the best. Nevertheless, their mere percentage cannot define the strength of their mind.

Numbers, in reality, are not verdicts, but evidence. They give us a reason to believe something, but not a reason to put our complete trust in it. Their evidence should inform your judgment rather than replace it. Along with this, context, circumstances, individual differences, creativity, judgement, and other qualities can be difficult to represent numerically. Ignoring them simply because they are harder to measure can give us an incomplete understanding. Numbers don’t necessarily stop being accurate, for they depend on the way we interpret them. It is our responsibility to do it correctly. Thank you for reading Social Science 43, and have a numerical day.
