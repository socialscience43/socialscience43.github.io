Arrival Fallacy: The Brutality of Success

Social Science 43 · November 21, 2026

“The cost of a thing is the amount of what I will call life which is required to be exchanged for it, immediately or in the long run.”
— Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854)

We often view success itself as the gold standard for where we want our life to be. We assume there is nothing better than it. The moment we achieve all of our goals, we will be in a state of existential bliss. Life would be in complete fulfillment, in a perfect state. However, there is a catch we must consider. Imagine that the difficulty of a goal is a fixed cost. You can pay it all up front, in the years of struggle before you succeed, or you can pay it later, in the new responsibilities and pressures that come with achievement. You may even split it between the two. But the total cost tends to stay the same. So reaching the goal doesn't make the burden disappear. It only changes its form, and we may feel empty. In a way, the longing for the goal was more satisfying than the goal itself. Does this make the fulfillment of success impossible to reach? How then would we measure success itself if not by the fulfillment?

First, we must consider the idea of the arrival fallacy. This is the false belief that reaching a major goal or milestone will bring you lasting happiness or fulfillment. It was popularized by psychologist Tal Ben-Shahar, and shows that what may truly follow are emptiness, disappointment, and worst of all, the endless chase for the next target. The chase will wear you out and deprive you of meaning when you begin forgetting to recognize the greatness of your achievements. This connects to the idea of the hedonic treadmill, which is the tendency to return to a baseline level of happiness no matter what we achieve. When searching for more after reaching a goal, the true meaning of the achievement itself quickly fades into dust. The goals become a temporary satisfaction, rather than a sense of deep-down fulfillment.

Returning to the previously posed question, does this make the fulfillment of success impossible to reach? This all depends upon the way we measure success, which can be a difficult thing to do as well. If we define it by simply reaching the goals we have set for ourselves, how can this actually be fulfilling? Once we have achieved something, our expectations change. The extraordinary becomes the ordinary. Another goal takes its place. This is actually quite similar to what I call collectors’ fatigue. When a major watch collector receives a new watch, it may feel amazing at first, but can quickly fade into just another watch in their major collection. Ambition can become an endless cycle if we fail to recognize what our achievements mean.

With these thoughts established, we return to the philosophy of Thoreau. The fixed cost we mentioned earlier is exactly what Thoreau describes. As we achieve success in one area, we may sacrifice something in another. We may work exceptionally hard to achieve something, and lose time for leisure or our overall morale. These losses may not look like part of the work, but they matter more than you think. Low morale can actually reduce your willingness to work, and ultimately undermine your progress. Neglecting personal well-being can make sustained effort more difficult. For example, someone who sacrifices sleep and health for a promotion often finds their work suffering. If you overwork yourself, it can be harmful to the results you want to achieve. We often can't lower the total cost of success, but we can choose how and when we pay it, and what we're willing to give up. If we pay with our relationships and our health, the success may feel hollow. If we pay in ways we can live with, it can feel real. Maybe it was never about how much you reached, but how you reached it. Thank you for reading Social Science 43, and have a fulfilling day.
