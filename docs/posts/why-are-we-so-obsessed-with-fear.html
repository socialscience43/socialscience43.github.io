Why Are We So Obsessed With Fear?

Social Science 43 · October 31, 2026

“He who fears he shall suffer, already suffers what he fears.”
— Michel de Montaigne

Good day everyone, and welcome back to Social Science 43. Today, as those who celebrate it already know, is Halloween. In theme with the day, we are going to be reflecting on humanity’s fascination with fear. Every year during October, something unusual happens. People begin surrounding themselves with all sorts of movies and activities they would typically avoid. Haunted houses open their doors, horror movies fill theaters, and children dress up as monsters. For just the season, fear becomes entertainment and frightening becomes fascinating. Yet this raises a question for us: why do we deliberately seek out an emotion we would normally want to avoid?

There are multiple explanations to consider when trying to answer this question. One of the biggest reasons people enjoy frightening experiences is the suspense that accompanies them. When something is keeping you at the edge of your seat, you naturally want to know what will happen next, frightening or not. This element is used often in movies and haunted houses, and is what raises your heart rate. Along with this, the excitement of being afraid in movies and haunted houses is similar to that of a roller coaster. Even though you may dread what is in front of you beforehand, the thrill and exhilaration that happens during the event keeps you going back.

Another thought to consider is our fascination with the unknown. Humans have always been curious about what they don’t know, and what they cannot explain. Something about the ambiguity of it creates a sense of fear. For example, when you look into a place during the daytime, it usually won’t scare you because you can see and explain everything around you. However, when you look into the same place during the night, a fear arises. You know the place well because you’ve seen it during the day, yet you are still afraid because anything could be lurking in the darkness. Even when you are positive there isn’t, your lack of knowing will fill the darkness with possibilities.

There is an important distinction to make when considering the psychology behind all of this. We become much less afraid and curious when safety isn’t guaranteed. For example, a horror movie or a haunted house may terrify us, but we still know we will be safe once we reach the end. There isn’t normally a threat of being hurt. However, in an environment where safety isn’t guaranteed, the fun and excitement is lost. These are the types of situations we try to avoid.

Fear, in reality, is created in our minds. Our imagination allows us to experience it before anything truly scary has even happened. Sometimes, even the anticipation and suspense for something to happen is scarier than the actual thing that happens. Perhaps our fascination with fear has less to do with the frightening itself, and more to do with our curiosity about what we cannot understand. So with this, are we truly fascinated by fear, or simply by the unknown that lies behind it? Thank you for reading Social Science 43, and have a spooky day.
