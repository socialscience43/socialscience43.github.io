The Ship Is Sinking

Social Science 43 · September 19, 2026

“The greatest danger to our future is apathy.”
— Jane Goodall

Not too long ago, I saw a speech made by Telegram CEO Pavel Durov. In the speech, he used the sinking of the Titanic as a metaphor for the current state of global personal freedom and privacy. Those who were on the ship were so sure that it was unsinkable that they treated the iceberg like a minor inconvenience. It was not until it became too late for most that they evacuated. The most frightening part was not necessarily the moment when everyone realized it was sinking. It was everything that had happened beforehand.

Problems rarely first appear to us at their worst. They usually begin as something that seems small enough to dismiss. The reality, though, is that a warning becomes an inconvenience. An inconvenience becomes a problem. A problem becomes a crisis. And by the time that everyone agrees something must be done, the real opportunity to prevent it may already be gone. We become accustomed to problems quickly, which makes the real danger not the problem itself but the way we treat it.

Our familiarity with something can make it feel normal, even when something is clearly wrong. Just because something has been a certain way for a long time doesn’t make it right. It just makes it something that we are used to. We don’t always act against these things because action doesn’t feel necessary until consequences become obvious. We often wait for certainty before responding to uncertainty.

Acting before a problem becomes obvious, however, can be difficult. When there are no immediate consequences, taking action can feel unnecessary and wrong. Most of the time, we question the person raising the alarm before questioning the problem itself. Why worry about something that has not happened yet? Why change something that seems to be working? This kind of complacency is what makes disasters happen. But prevention rarely feels urgent. That is what makes it so difficult. By the time prevention becomes urgent, it is no longer prevention.

We cannot prevent every problem. Likewise, every warning may not turn out to be correct. However, that doesn’t mean warnings should be ignored simply because consequences haven’t yet occurred. The goal is not to live in fear of every possible disaster, nor is it to completely disregard them. It is to recognize that waiting for certainty can sometimes mean waiting until there is nothing left to prevent. The ship does not begin sinking when everyone agrees that it is sinking. It begins when the water first gets in. Thank you for reading Social Science 43, and stay afloat.
